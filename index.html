KNOWLEDGE BASE

Understand the Art, Not Just the Moves

Everything a student — or a curious visitor — needs to understand JKA Shotokan: where it came from, how the Japanese terms work, and the philosophy underneath the technique.

1868–1922
Karate Leaves Okinawa
Gichin Funakoshi, an Okinawan schoolteacher trained in the local fighting art of "te," is invited to demonstrate karate in Tokyo. He formally introduces the art to mainland Japan and begins renaming it "karate" — using characters meaning "empty hand."
1936
The Shotokan Dojo Opens
Funakoshi's students build a dedicated dojo in Tokyo, named "Shotokan" after his pen name, "Shoto" (meaning "pine waves"). The style that would come to be known as Shotokan karate takes institutional shape.
1949
The Japan Karate Association is Founded
The JKA is formally established in Tokyo to preserve and standardize Funakoshi's teachings, with Masatoshi Nakayama taking on the role of Chief Instructor after Funakoshi's passing in 1957 — codifying the technical curriculum still used today.
1950s–1970s
Global Expansion
The JKA sends senior instructors abroad as part of its overseas instructor program, spreading standardized Shotokan karate across the world and establishing national and regional federations.
2024–Present
A New Generation Leads JKA World Federation
Following the passing of longtime Chief Instructor Masaaki Ueki, 10th Dan, in July 2024, Oishi Shuseki Shihan, 9th Dan, now leads as Chief Instructor of JKA World Federation — carrying the technical standard forward into the next generation of practitioners worldwide.
Present Day

KAMAE — Stances

Zenkutsu-dachiFront stance
Long, low forward stance with weight loaded toward the front leg — the base for most attacking techniques.
Kiba-dachiStraddle-leg stance
Wide horse-riding stance, weight evenly split, used for lateral power and side techniques.
Kokutsu-dachiBack stance
Weight held mostly on the rear leg, front leg light — a defensive, evasive stance.

UKE — Blocks

Age-ukeRising block
Deflects a strike upward and away from the head.
Soto-ukeOutside block
Sweeps an attack from the centerline outward, arm moving in-to-out.
Gedan-baraiDownward sweep
Clears a low attack, typically a kick, sweeping downward across the body.

TSUKI & UCHI — Strikes

Oi-zukiLunge punch
A stepping punch thrown with the same-side hand as the forward leg.
Gyaku-zukiReverse punch
A punch thrown with the hand opposite the forward leg — the core scoring technique in kumite.
Shuto-uchiKnife-hand strike
A strike delivered with the blade edge of an open hand.

GERI — Kicks

Mae-geriFront kick
A snapping or thrusting kick delivered straight ahead with the ball of the foot.
Mawashi-geriRoundhouse kick
A kick that travels in an arc, striking with the instep or ball of the foot.
Yoko-geriSide kick
A kick thrust directly sideways, striking with the blade of the foot.

The Dojo Kun are five precepts recited at the close of every JKA class, attributed to Gichin Funakoshi. They frame karate as a discipline of character before a system of combat.

01
人格完成に努むること
Hitotsu, jinkaku kansei ni tsutomuru koto
Seek Perfection of Character
02
誠の道を守ること
Hitotsu, makoto no michi wo mamoru koto
Be Faithful
03
努力の精神を養うこと
Hitotsu, doryoku no seishin wo yashinau koto
Endeavour
04
礼儀を重んずること
Hitotsu, reigi wo omonzuru koto
Respect Others
05
血気の勇を戒むること
Hitotsu, kekki no yū wo imashimuru koto
Refrain from violent behaviour

Karate ni sente nashi — "There is no first attack in karate." One of Funakoshi's twenty guiding precepts (Niju Kun), and the philosophical center of JKA training: karate exists for defense and self-mastery, never for provocation.
